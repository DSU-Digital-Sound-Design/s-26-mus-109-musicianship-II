Waxahatchee — "Fire" (Saint Cloud, 2020) Critical Listening Analysis — April 19, 2026
Produced by Brad Cook. Engineered by Jerry Ordonez, with additional engineering by Jon Low. Mixed by Jon Low. Mastered by Brent Lambert at The Kitchen. Recorded at Sonic Ranch, Tornillo, TX and Long Pond, Stuyvesant, NY. Personnel: Katie Crutchfield (vocals, acoustic guitar, piano, keys), Brad Cook (bass, acoustic guitar, piano, keys, synth), Bobby Colombo (electric guitar, acoustic guitar, keys), Bill Lennox (electric guitar, acoustic guitar, keys, percussion), Nick Kinsey (drums, percussion), Josh Kaufman (electric guitars, piano, organ, percussion).
 
"Fire" is the third track on Saint Cloud, Waxahatchee's fifth album, released March 27, 2020 on Merge Records. Produced by Brad Cook — known for his work with Bon Iver and Nathaniel Rateliff — and mixed by Jon Low at Long Pond in upstate New York, the record was tracked at Sonic Ranch in Tornillo, Texas, a large residential studio complex built around a vintage Neve 8078 console. The band assembled for the sessions included members of the Detroit group Bonny Doon, multi-instrumentalist Josh Kaufman, and drummer Nick Kinsey. Saint Cloud marked a deliberate shift in Crutchfield's sound — away from the distorted indie rock of its predecessor Out in the Storm and toward a sparser, more naturalistic Americana. "Fire" exemplifies that approach. The arrangement is spare, the production unforced, and the mix organized around Crutchfield's voice. What follows is a critical listening analysis of the track across spatial, timbral, dynamic, and transparency dimensions, drawing on both Jason Corey's critical listening framework and the EBU Tech 3286 subjective evaluation parameters.
"Fire" opens with a warm, Rhodes-style keyboard — likely the Yamaha Reface CS — playing alone. Two synthesizer drones enter gradually beneath it: one with a string-like timbre spanning the upper registers, one sitting just beneath that, sharing its droned rhythm. By the time Verse 1 arrives, these three elements have established the harmonic and timbral foundation the rest of the song will inhabit. Underneath all of it, a low, constant noise floor is audible — microphone or preamp noise from the recording chain, confirmed consistent across multiple playback systems. Rather than a flaw, this hiss signals the record's intent: nothing has been scrubbed clean. The production, by Brad Cook at Sonic Ranch in Tornillo, Texas, and Long Pond in Stuyvesant, New York, wears its naturalism openly.

The arrangement builds in discrete steps. As the loudness graph shows, the track begins at pianissimo and rises through each section — piano at the intro, mezzo-piano into Verse 1, mezzo-forte by Verse 2, and forte at Verse 4 — before falling back to mezzo-piano in the outro and approaching its opening level by the final bars. This arc is carried entirely by arrangement density. There is no chorus, no formal contrast — only verses, each adding instruments and weight.
The intro establishes two synthesizer drones: one with a string-like timbre spanning the upper registers, one sitting just beneath it. As the frequency spectrum diagram shows, the string synth occupies a wide band from mid to high frequencies, spreading across the full stereo width. These drones persist throughout the song, audible in the gaps between louder instruments and otherwise absorbed into the texture. They are easy to miss. Their constancy gives the track a quiet harmonic foundation that rewards close listening. A warm, Fender Rhodes-style keyboard anchors the midrange center. When the drums enter at Verse 1, the kick arrives alone, followed by two dry Telecaster guitars — one melodic, one chordal — panned hard left and right. Hi-hat enters slightly left of center; snare slightly right. Vocals, bass, and double-tracked harmonies accumulate through Verses 2 and 3. Toms arrive in Verse 4. A background vocal enters in the outro.

The kick and toms anchor the low end on the right, while bass — shown on the left panel as a wide low-frequency block — is present but recessed. The high hat marks the top of the spectrum. This is a mid-forward mix by design. The quiet bass cedes the low register to the kick, which is punchy and subby with a controlled short decay and an audible transient click. The snare is snappy and thin-bodied, its character coming from the wire rather than the shell. Guitars are bright and jangly. The keyboard is warm and dark. Crutchfield's vocal is intimate and clear.
Loudness balance confirms these priorities. The vocal predominates. Kick and snare sit forward. Keyboards are noticeably present. Guitars occupy the middle ground. Bass is quiet — deliberately so, reinforcing the high-register emphasis and opening space for the kick to define the low end alone. Dynamic range is natural; nothing sounds compressed. The mix has the quality of a live performance captured rather than constructed, consistent with the Neve 8078 tracking chain and Jon Low's analog-leaning mix approach.
All sources are identifiable and distinguishable. The lower drone synthesizer is the one exception — it disappears beneath the full arrangement and surfaces only in the silences, a mix decision that serves atmosphere over audibility. Vocal text is fully intelligible throughout. No distortions, clicks, or artifacts appear beyond the noise floor noted above, which rates a 5 on the EBU scale: perceptible but not annoying, and arguably appropriate to the recording's character.
The production chain is audible in the result. Tracked through a Neve 8078, mixed by Jon Low with hardware outboard, mastered by Brent Lambert — the record sounds warm, deep, and unforced. The hypothesis holds. This is a record that sounds like people in a room together, and the mix preserves that impression without sentimentalizing it.
The spatial, timbral, and dynamic dimensions of "Fire" form a coherent and mutually reinforcing whole. The guitars — the brightest and most spectrally aggressive elements in the mix — are panned hard left and right, keeping the center clear for the vocals and keyboards. Had they been placed centrally, they would compete directly with the instruments and voices that carry the song's emotional weight. The same logic applies to the backing vocals, which are similarly displaced from center to avoid muddying the lead. Panning here is not decorative; it is the primary tool by which transparency is achieved. The arrangement is sparse enough that each instrument can occupy its own space without masking, and the mix is organized to protect that separation.
The most intentional production decision is the treatment of the low end. The bass is receded to a degree unusual for the instrumentation — quiet enough that the kick drum takes full ownership of the low register. This is surprising. In most recordings of this genre, the bass and kick share the low end in roughly equal measure. Here, the kick is prominent and punchy while the bass recedes, shifting the center of gravity upward and reinforcing the mid-forward spectral character that defines the mix. The result is a recording that feels simultaneously rhythmically grounded and tonally light — driven from below by the kick, but warm and open rather than heavy.
