## Waxahatchee, "Fire"

- *Saint Cloud* (2020)
- Critical listening analysis
- Space, timbre, dynamics, and transparency
- One full listen, then sectional listening

{{% note %}}

- Treat this as a full critical listening session, not a lecture.
- We hear the whole track once, then return to sections and connect what we hear to the diagrams.
- The argument: the track sounds natural and unforced, but it is not simple. The arrangement and mix are meticulous.
- Listen for four things the whole hour: the centered vocal, the restrained bass, the hard-panned guitars, and the outro's spatial opening.

{{%/ note %}}

---

## First Listen

- Listen straight through before analyzing
- Track the overall dynamic arc
- Notice what inhabits the center of the mix
- Listen for what changes from verse to verse

{{% note %}}

- Listening cue: play the track through without interruption.
- Tell them not to solve it yet. Just get the shape.
- The song builds without a big chorus release. It grows by accumulating layers.
- After the playthrough, ask for first impressions: What felt closest? What felt widest? What felt surprisingly quiet?
- The listen runs just over four minutes; allow two or three minutes of reactions before moving on.

{{%/ note %}}

## Production Context

- Track 3 on *Saint Cloud*, released March 27, 2020
- Produced by [Brad Cook](https://www.bradleytime.com/discography-1); mixed by [Jon Low](https://www.allmusic.com/artist/jon-low-mn0003903871)
- Tracked at [Sonic Ranch](https://www.sonicranch.com/) and [Long Pond Studio](https://www.to-form.com/long-pond-recording-studio)
- Shift toward sparse, naturalistic Americana

{{% note %}}

- This slide sets up why the recording sounds the way it does.
- Katie Crutchfield moved away from the distorted indie rock of earlier Waxahatchee records toward an open, roots-oriented sound on *Saint Cloud*.
- Brad Cook is central. The production refuses to inflate the band or hyper-compress it. It protects the feeling of people playing together in a room.
- Don't dwell on credits, but connect production decisions to the sound they just heard.
- The Sonic Ranch / Long Pond combination explains the warm, analog-leaning feel.

{{%/ note %}}

## Intro and Verse 1

- Rhodes-style keyboard establishes the center
- Two synth drones create a quiet harmonic bed
- Kick enters first, then dry guitars hard left and right
- Verse 1 sets the spatial and timbral rules for the track

{{% note %}}

- Listening cue: play the intro through the first verse, then pause once the groove is established.
- Walk through the entrance order, because the arrangement is doing the mixing work for us.
- The keyboard anchors the center warmly. The drones are easy to miss but keep the texture alive underneath.
- The kick arrives alone and grounds the track. The two guitars then widen the image without cluttering the center.
- The mix is already teaching its priorities: center for voice and keys, edges for bright guitars, low end led by the kick.

{{%/ note %}}

{{% note %}}

- Figure 2 is the holistic environment diagram — the most important visual for the lecture.
- The mix feels spacious not because it is drenched in reverb, but because each source has a clear position and depth.
- The vocal is forward. Kick and snare sit centered but behind the vocal. The guitars live at the edges. The string synth acts like a rear wall.
- The space feels small to medium for most of the song, which keeps the track intimate.
- Listening cue: replay a middle verse where vocal, guitars, drums, and keys are all present, then ask them to describe front, middle, and back layers.

<div style="flex:1; text-align:left;">

- The mix is mid-forward by design
- Voice, guitars, keys, and snare share the midrange
- Kick defines the low end more than the bass does
- Figure 3 shows the width of the upper synth layer

</div>
</div>

{{% note %}}

- Figure 3 translates the sonic impression into frequency language.
- The track does not feel heavy. It feels warm and present because the energy concentrates in the mids and upper mids.
- Contrast the keyboard and the guitars: the keyboard is darker and warmer; the guitars are brighter and jangly.
- The string synth does hidden work. It spreads broadly across the upper texture, but because it is quiet, students usually miss it until prompted.
- Listening cue: replay a late-verse section and ask them to listen for the bright guitar edges against the darker keyboard in the center.

{{%/ note %}}

## Low End and Dynamics

- The kick is punchy, short, and dominant
- The bass is present but deliberately recessed
- Dynamic range feels natural, not over-compressed
- The track stays grounded without sounding heavy

{{% note %}}

- This is one of the most interesting production choices in the song.
- In most Americana or rock-adjacent recordings, bass and kick share the low end evenly. Here the kick owns the low register.
- That choice shifts the center of gravity upward. The song feels rhythmically solid but not thick or bass-heavy.
- It also preserves vocal clarity. A lighter bass presence leaves more room for the midrange.
- Listening cue: replay a section with full drums and bass, then ask whether the low end feels bass-led or kick-led before answering.

{{%/ note %}}

---

## Transparency and Noise Floor

- Nearly every source remains easy to identify
- Vocal text stays intelligible throughout
- A constant hiss sits underneath the track
- The noise floor supports the record's naturalism

{{% note %}}

- Don't frame the hiss as a mistake. It fits the aesthetic and tells us the production didn't scrub the track into digital silence.
- Transparency is otherwise strong. We can pick out almost every source, even as the arrangement densifies.
- The partial exception is the lower drone, which recedes into the texture and becomes atmospheric rather than explicit.
- Ask students whether they hear the hiss as a flaw, a neutral artifact, or part of the record's character.

{{%/ note %}}

---

## The Outro Changes the Space

- Background vocal enters behind the lead
- Reverb opens up more than earlier sections
- The track ends looser, wider, and more reflective
- The room expands without breaking continuity

{{% note %}}

- Listening cue: play the outro and let it run nearly to the end before talking.
- The outro doesn't just thin out dynamically. It changes the apparent room.
- Earlier sections feel contained and intimate. The outro adds a more open, reflective vocal space.
- A subtle but important move: it lifts the ending without abandoning the restrained aesthetic.
- Ask them whether the space feels like a hall, a chamber, or an expanded version of the earlier room.

{{%/ note %}}

## Takeaways and Discussion

- Sparse arrangements can still be highly detailed
- Panning does major transparency work here
- The kick-heavy low end reshapes the mix
- Naturalism here is a production choice, not an absence of craft

{{% note %}}

- Restate the core argument in plain language.
- The track sounds simple on first listen, but the arrangement and mix are disciplined.
- The hard-panned guitars protect the center. The restrained bass keeps the mids open. The vocal stays intimate. The outro widens the frame at the right moment.
- If there is time, ask which single decision mattered most: guitar placement, bass restraint, vocal intimacy, or the synth bed.
- Transition here into open discussion or a short writing response to fill the hour.
